Thank you all for coming tonight. Before we start, a little housekeeping. The coffee is at the back of the room, and the slides will be sent to everyone tomorrow morning.

I want to tell you about the worst day my bakery ever had. It was a Saturday in winter, and the oven broke before sunrise. We had a line of customers outside and nothing warm to sell. I stood at the door and told every person the truth. I said the oven is broken, the bread is late, and I am sorry. Nobody left angry. Most of them came back the next day. That morning taught me that customers forgive bad news. What they do not forgive is silence.

Now let me talk about price, because almost everyone gets it wrong. Most small shops set their price by looking at the shop across the street. That is a mistake. Your neighbor does not know your costs, and your neighbor is probably losing money too. Start with what the bread costs you to make. Add the rent, the flour, the power, and your own time. Then add enough so that a slow week does not close your doors. If a customer says your bread is too expensive, do not argue. Smile, and let them go. The customers who stay are the ones who keep you open.

Here is something nobody told me about hiring. The best baker I ever hired had never baked before. She had worked in a hospital kitchen for many years. She knew how to show up on time, how to keep a clean table, and how to stay calm when everything went wrong. I could teach her bread in a month. I could never have taught her that calm. So when you hire, stop looking for skill. Look for the habits that are hard to teach, and teach the rest yourself.

I also want to be honest about a mistake that almost ended the business. A big hotel asked us to deliver bread every morning. The order was huge, and I said yes before I finished reading the contract. Within a few weeks the hotel was most of our income. Then they asked for a lower price, and I had no choice. I had let a single customer become my boss. It took a full year to get free. My rule today is simple. No customer is allowed to be more than a small slice of what we earn. When one of them grows too large, I go and find others before I need them.

People often ask me how to know when it is time to grow. My answer is that growth should feel boring. If opening another shop feels like an adventure, you are not ready. You are ready when the shop you have runs without you for a whole month, and nothing breaks. Take a real holiday and turn off your phone. If you come back and the bread is still good, you have built a business. If you come back to a fire, you have only built a job for yourself.

Let me finish with the smallest lesson, which may be the most useful. Write things down. Every recipe, every supplier, every little trick that lives only in your head. The day you get sick, or tired, or simply want to leave early, that notebook is the difference between a shop that opens and a shop that stays dark. I wrote ours on a rainy afternoon, and it has saved us more times than I can count.

That is everything I have for you tonight. Thank you for listening, and thank you to the library for giving us this room. Please stay for a coffee, and come and say hello.
