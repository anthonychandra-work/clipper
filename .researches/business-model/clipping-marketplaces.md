# Clipping marketplaces

## The model

A streamer, creator, brand or music label funds a campaign with a budget and a rate per 1,000 views. Anyone can join, cut clips from the campaign's source content, and post them to their own TikTok, Reels or Shorts accounts. The marketplace verifies the view counts and pays the clipper from the campaign budget. The clipper needs no audience and no application.

The buyer pays only for views delivered, spread across hundreds of accounts that carry no ad label. The clipper carries the risk: no views, no pay.

Two variants exist alongside the per-view model. Affiliate campaigns pay a commission on sales the clips drive. Direct hire pays a retainer of $500–$1,500 a month or $5–$20 per delivered clip.

One tracker of Whop payouts calculated a blended rate of about $0.39 per 1,000 views once rejected clips and caps are counted, against the $1.25 advertised average. Agencies that run clipper teams keep 20–80% of payouts.

## Scale

Whop is the largest marketplace. Tether invested $200M in it at a $1.6B valuation on 25 February 2026. Forbes reported in April 2026 that Content Rewards pays out more than $40,000 a day across nearly a million videos a month. A podcast interview put it at 400,000–500,000 participating creators and 30–40 billion views a month.

One tracker counted $2.58M paid to 8,466 clippers across 6.6 billion views, with $887,000 of that in February 2026 alone. That works out to about $305 per clipper over their lifetime on the platform.

A 30-day census by CreatorDB found 9,941 hashtagged campaign clips from 1,820 accounts, which drew 1.68 billion YouTube views and 260 million TikTok plays.

## Buyers

Streamers are the largest buyers. N3on paid $1.4M to 303 clippers in five weeks at $40–$50 per 100,000 views. Kai Cenat, Adin Ross, IShowSpeed, xQc and CaseOh run campaigns, and Kick reportedly subsidises clippers itself. MrBeast paid more than $100,000 for clips of Beast Games. Other named buyers include Polymarket, ElevenLabs and Justin Bieber.

## What clippers earn

Blogs that sell tools to clippers give these ranges: $100–$500 a month for beginners, $1,000–$3,000 for someone posting 5–15 clips a day across several campaigns, and $10,000 or more for the top 1% running many accounts. The tracker average of $305 lifetime per clipper puts the typical participant far below those ranges.

## Risks for clippers

- Campaign owners can reject a clip after its views have accrued.
- Bot detection flags legitimate accounts; disputed payouts are held for up to 90 days.
- Campaign budgets run out mid-flight.

## Risks for the model

**Disclosure.** Paid clips carry no advertising label on any platform. CreatorDB notes that the disclosure rules regulators attached to influencer marketing do not reach clippers, and that gambling-stream clips reach minors without restriction.

**Platform policy.** YouTube's monetisation policy against mass-produced, templated content was renamed "inauthentic content" in July 2025 and, by one report, "generic or repetitive content" in July 2026. It suspends monetisation for the whole channel. YouTube's separate reused-content policy allows clips and compilations where the uploader adds original value. Marketplace payouts come from the campaign budget, not from platform ad revenue, so the monetisation policy does not cut a clipper's marketplace income directly.

## Reliability of these sources

Most published numbers on this economy come from blogs run by clipping tools and clipping marketplaces, which have an interest in recruiting clippers. The Tether investment, the Forbes payout figure and the CreatorDB census are the only figures here from outside that group.
